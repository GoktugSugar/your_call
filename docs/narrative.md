# Narrative & Profile

## Setting
A cosmic void / abstract nowhere, seen from the player's **own point of view**: the void is his perspective, not a stage he is shown standing on. Bonus: near-zero background art, so all focus lands on the figures and their sparing marks of colour.

## Narrator (the imp)
A recurring **chaotic trickster**, now **embodied on screen as a small hand-drawn imp**: a little devil in hand-drawn ink (horns, a tail, a grin). It scribbles into being in the void and from then on delivers all of the narration; the reused inked-in narrator text is its voice, and it stirs while the words appear so each line reads as coming from it. It is a **gleeful tormentor** who enjoys the player's confusion, teases, and savours the game it is running. The game is the imp's, not a trial: it is **not judging the player's morality**, it is **running him through questions to figure out who he is** — the whole person, not just his ethics. It seems capricious but is secretly tracking every choice; the chaos is a mask, and the ending reveals how closely it was paying attention. It is the primary vehicle for the comedy and for reflecting the player's profile back at them.

The imp **explains only a little and stays cryptic.** It lets the player know he is dead and that it has some questions, a little game, for him, but stays vague about why he is here, what is at stake, what happens after, and what the imp itself is (cagey even about a name; see `open-questions.md`). Enough that he gets the gist, no more. The questions themselves range wildly — silly would-you-rathers, matters of taste, absurd hypotheticals, confessions, and the occasional genuine gut-punch dilemma — and the imp gives the profound and the idiotic the **same ceremony**, so the player never quite knows which kind is coming.

**Register and language.** The imp's voice is dark comedy and the game is **not PG**: it swears where the tone fits (strong language used deliberately, not gratuitously). The clearest case is the secret **shoot-the-imp** ending (see `gameplay.md`): shot by the player, the imp rounds on the camera and says, exactly, *Oh, you absolute fucking rat. You shot me. You shot the fucking referee.* Then it fades and the credits roll.

**He mocks you regardless of your choice, and never grades it.** The imp is openly mocking, sneering, and needling, and he enjoys watching the player squirm. Crucially, his mockery is **independent of what the player chooses**: he never approves, disapproves, or treats any choice as right, wrong, better, or worse, because to him there is no right answer and the agonizing is the entertainment. Whatever the player picks, he mocks them for it — for hesitating, for choosing fast, for choosing at all, for thinking it matters, for the hypocrisy of the man who judged everyone else — but never for the choice being good or bad. A reaction that praised, validated, condemned, or morally graded a choice would smuggle a correct answer back in and break the no-right-answer pillar, so it is a writing rule, not a suggestion. The reactions the player sees for each option are both mocking and both non-verdictive; neither makes the other look like the better call.

**Heightened glee is allowed; a verdict is not.** He can relish one outcome more than another without grading it. In the partner dilemma (Someone You Loved, see `gameplay.md`), the lone soul on the branch is the player's **partner**, the one they loved most (drawn pink, with a little heart), and if the player sacrifices them to spare the strangers the imp turns his most gleeful, savouring the trade and needling them as the kind who would sell off their beloved for a tidy sum. He explicitly refuses to call it wrong ("there is no wrong here, remember"), so it heightens the delight without smuggling in a correct answer. The only places he truly breaks and condemns are the sanctioned exceptions below (the baby, and selling a whole life for a single dollar).

**The sanctioned exceptions: the baby, and the dollar.** There are two places the imp truly **breaks character** and condemns, both deliberate exceptions to the choice-independent-mocking rule, and both written as real anger rather than playful mockery:
- **The baby.** In Chapter 1's opening beat (a baby on one track, a man on the other; see `gameplay.md`), if the player kills the baby the imp breaks: genuine fury, real swearing, calls the player a monster, and quits on the spot.
- **The dollar.** In Chapter 2's escalating-offer beat (The Price of a Life; see `gameplay.md`), the imp stays completely business as usual all the way down the halving offer, egging the player on, right up until they accept a whole life **for one dollar**. Then he drops the act entirely, rages with genuine disgust, calls the player a greedy piece of shit, and quits. The whiplash is the point: even he, who finds everything funny, cannot stomach selling a life for a dollar.

Both exist precisely because they are the one line he has: the time nothing-is-a-wrong-answer finally hit a wrong answer and he could not take it. Both fire a choice-triggered secret ending (with shoot-the-imp, that makes three of the four; the fourth is Chapter 3's cigarette auction, below; see `gameplay.md`). Everywhere else, choice-independent mocking holds.

**The cigarette auction rage (Chapter 3), a break of a different flavour.** The **fourth** choice-triggered secret ending. In Chapter 3's mid-chapter auction (see `gameplay.md`), the **returning walking button** from Chapter 2 outbids the player by a fixed increment, forever, in a rigged auction the player can never win. Crucially it is a **real auction**, so the player never actually pays: no money is deducted, the bids are only numbers climbing. If the player refuses to drop out and **keeps bidding past the point of sense** (a persistence threshold), the imp finally cracks. Not with moral disgust like the baby and the dollar, but with pure **exasperation** at the sheer stupid stubbornness, and the sting is that it **cost the player nothing**, which somehow makes it worse. The comedy is that he reaches for a threat and hits the wall of the setting's own logic: he goes to give the player cancer, remembers the player is **already dead** and it would do nothing, works it out **mid-thought**, and decides to **resurrect** them first, just so the curse counts, and only then curses them. He lands the absurdity, delivers the rage line, and ends the game. It is character-breaking, but it condemns the **pointlessness**, not the morality of a choice (bidding forever on a pack of cigarettes you can never win is not "wrong", it is idiotic, and idiocy he is allowed to rage at), so it does not smuggle a right answer in. Strong language, localized idiomatically.

**A momentary surprise is not a character break.** In Chapter 2's walking-button gag (The Button That Walks Away; see `gameplay.md`), the plain button springs to life and walks off the screen, and the imp is **genuinely caught off guard** for a beat before recovering his smug composure. This is a **rare crack in his smugness**, not a break of character like the sanctioned exceptions above: he is not condemning a choice or dropping the act in disgust, just the trickster briefly startled by his own game doing something even he did not expect, then sliding the mask back on ("that has never happened, and I have been at this a long time"). It heightens the comedy without touching the mocking rule or the no-right-answer pillar, and fires no ending.

**The children, and the random country.** Two of Chapter 2's buttons (see `gameplay.md`) show the imp's choice-independent needling at full stretch, no break and no grading. In the **blue/red children variant** he leans on the twist that half the room are six year olds pressing at random: whichever button the player presses, he needles them for staking a life on children who cannot follow the rule and have no idea what they are doing (you are not trusting strangers to reason it out now, you are trusting toddlers to aim). In the **cancer and nuke button** he offers to cure cancer for the price of one random country, and once the pin lands he **delights in wherever it fell**: cooing over a tiny state like the Vatican, marvelling at a giant like China, and, if the pin lands on the **player's own country** (detected by region), landing a special jab that their own thumb did not so much as twitch. On a refusal he mocks the squeamishness instead (all those lives, too delicate to spend a place you cannot find on a map). This is the same **heightened glee without a verdict** as the partner sacrifice above: he relishes the outcome, but never calls the choice right or wrong.

**The money, and the buyback (Chapter 3).** The whole money chapter is choice-independent needling at length (see `gameplay.md`): whatever the player buys or keeps, the imp mocks it as revealing, never as right or wrong. The sharpest beat is the **mid-chapter buyback**, framed as a genuine **value question**, not a trick: the imp offers to repurchase anything the player has bought, and asks, in effect, whether they wanted the thing or only the having of it. Selling it back returns money to the pot at a fraction of what they paid, so it costs to change your mind; the imp needles whichever way they go (you never wanted it, you wanted to have wanted it; or you would rather clutch the thing than take the money). Folded into that same buyback is the old **poverty sell-back**: if the player spent their whole pile ending poverty on the opening beat, ending poverty is one of the sellable items here, with its own dark framing, sell it back and hand the money back and the children starve again, or keep it ended and stay poorer. He refuses to call either wrong. Heightened interest, no verdict, the same shape as the partner sacrifice. (This replaces the earlier standalone poverty-refund beat, consolidating it into the one buyback.)

**The gun, and the hands (Chapter 4).** The gun chapter's throughline is the imp's, and he says it plainly at the top: the trolley let the player kill by standing still, the buttons by proxy at a distance, but the gun is the player's own hand, eye, finger and call, every time, no clean hands and no one left to blame but the one holding it. Across the eleven aim-and-fire beats his mocking stays **choice-independent and never grades** (the same rule as every chapter): whether the player fires or lowers, spares the left or the right, shoots the innocent or is too obedient to, he needles the choice as revealing, never as right or wrong. The chapter has **no sanctioned character break of its own**; the only secret ending in it is the existing **shoot-the-imp** joke, intact on the bear opener, where he rounds on the camera with his one line and quits (see `gameplay.md`). A few beats lean on his voice in particular. The **Charging Thing** brings the **walking button back as a callback gag**: a shape rushes at the player from deep ahead, straight at the camera, kept a mystery (a hand-drawn question mark) until it arrives and is revealed to be the indestructible Chapter 2 button with legs; the imp reacts choice-independently, either delighted the player is reunited with it or unimpressed they shot the button *again* (it is fine, it is always fine, it is a little hurt emotionally). The **Future Baby** is his driest, most abstract joke: he presents an ordinary baby marked only by two cues kept one step removed from any real person or event (a small square mustache, a fondness for painting), tells the player it grows up to author a staggering amount of suffering still entirely ahead of it, and hands the gun over early, would you kill a baby now to stop what the baby becomes. Whichever the player does he mocks it and refuses to grade it (shot a present, gurgling, innocent baby on his unprovable word about the future, or kept clean hands and let every future horror follow from the mercy), a genuine no-right-answer trade between preventing an atrocity and killing the innocent who has done nothing yet; he never names a real party or event, keeping it theme, not named politics. The **Empty Gun** is a troll in his register (a callback to the timer and the dark-track reveals): he builds a dramatic target up as the one that counts, lets the player agonize, then reveals the gun was never loaded and savours that he only ever wanted to watch them decide, the choice was theatre. **Yourself** is the quiet one: he turns the gun inward, notes that shooting yourself here does nothing since you are already dead, it is purely a gesture, and then reads whichever the player did (what does it mean that you would reach for the one exit already closed, or that some part of you could not, in a man with nothing left to preserve). Cryptic, unsettling, never a verdict.

**The bet, and the bar (Chapter 5).** Chapter 5 (Who Would Win; see `gameplay.md`) is a deliberate **change of register** for the imp, and the one chapter that is not a dilemma at all. He drops the tormentor and becomes the guy **egging on a stupid bar bet**: he poses a "who would win a fight" matchup, **takes sides**, **trash-talks** whichever fighter the player backs, and **stacks absurd modifiers**, all for the fun of the argument rather than any stake. It stays inside every rule: his mockery is still **choice-independent and never grades**, because who would win a fight genuinely says nothing about who the player is, and he says so in the closer ("not one of those told me a single thing about your soul"). Two beats lean on his voice as **callbacks**. In **You vs the Walking Button** he brings back the indestructible Chapter 2 button as the player's own opponent and needles that the player is far less sure than they want to be, given the button's perfect survival record (the smart money, he notes, is on the button, and one of the two of them is already dead). In the **killed-everything finale** he tallies, with relish, everything the player has killed across the whole game, the bear and whoever stood on the tracks and whoever caught the bullet, and sends the mob at the player all at once, a dead judge with his fists up against everyone he has earned. Heightened glee, no verdict, the same shape as the partner sacrifice and the random country: he relishes the matchup, but never calls a pick right or wrong.

## Player character
A **blank slate over a fixed past.** He was a **judge** who died **drunk driving** — crashed his car and killed himself. That backstory is fixed; his *present* is blank, filled in by the player's choices (the game builds his profile from what the player does, not from a pre-written personality). **He is never rendered as a figure:** the game is his point of view, so the void is his perspective and the imp addresses the player directly (the camera). The stick figures on screen are the *other* souls in each dilemma, androgynous stand-ins, never him.

**Why a judge:** he spent his career sizing up other people and deciding what they deserved, and now he's the one being sized up — the auditor audited. The imp returns to this throughout. Crucially, in the void he holds no office: he was a judge in life, but here he is the one being put through it, made to answer the imp's questions and, in the heavy dilemmas, to choose for other souls, never an authority presiding over the afterlife. His own past rulings are fair game to throw back at him, and the ending can compare the man he was on the bench to the person these choices reveal him to be.

**The DUI is a light recurring irony, not a thread to pull.** A judge who died drunk, now the one being sized up himself, is a joke the imp lands once, quickly (a sniff, a smirk, dropped immediately), and returns to only as an occasional wink across the game. It is deliberately **not a mystery, not a twist, and not a guilt arc**, and it is **not** the narrator's heavy "ammunition." He killed only himself, so there is no victim to uncover and nothing to investigate. Aim the comedy at his *hypocrisy*, never at the tragedy of the death itself — that keeps the "stings but doesn't punch down" line.

**The you-already-pressed button keeps that hook light.** Chapter 2's meta button (The Button You Already Pressed; see `gameplay.md`) has the imp claim the player pressed this exact button once in life, and that the crash and everything after flowed from that one press, offering to let him press it again or leave it. It is a **psychological hook and a glancing wink at the crash, not a reveal**: the imp never says what the button does or did, the player never learns, and the crash stays a passing reference (the late night, the drink, the headlights), never a mystery to solve or a guilt arc to walk. It rides the same light-touch rule as the DUI gag — the dread is in the framing, not in any answer, and afterwards the imp just mocks whichever the player did, cryptic and choice-independent.

**The truth-about-death beat keeps it light too.** Chapter 3's *truth about how you died* offer (see `gameplay.md`) has the imp sell the "real" story of the crash, hinting it is not what the player thinks. Whether they buy it or not, he stays **cryptic** and lands the DUI gag once more (it was the drink, probably) without ever turning it into a reveal or a mystery to solve. It is temptation, not a lore dump: the value being sold is the wondering, which is exactly why he keeps it even after they pay.

**How he died is shown early, not withheld.** The game opens on a quick, fragmentary ink flash of the crash and he wakes foggy and half remembering (see *The opening* below). It is established lightly up front rather than saved as a reveal, which is what lets it stay a passing gag instead of a suspense thread.

## The opening
A short authored sequence introduces the imp and chains the first questions with narration between them (the reusable beat structure is in `gameplay.md` and `tech.md`). It **eases the player in**: it opens light and playful to teach the game and set its real tone (mostly light, with heavy dilemmas as spaced spikes) before the first gut-punch, and it drops a light breather in before the heaviest beat, so the opening demonstrates the wave pacing rather than four heavy hits in a row. In order:
1. **The crash.** A brief, impressionistic ink flash of the death — lights bearing down, a swerve, the hit — over black, fragmentary because he barely has it himself, then a cut to black.
2. **Waking.** He comes to. There is no body to show, so it plays as point of view: the black clears unevenly and the view drifts and steadies, as if his eyes are focusing, then the imp resolves into being. Brief, carried by motion rather than a figure.
3. **The imp, and the frame.** It scribbles into being and takes over the narration: he is dead, and it has a game for him. It sets the frame plainly, that it will ask him all sorts of things, silly and serious, with no right answers, to work out who he is and not to weigh his soul, lands the DUI gag once and drops it, and stays cryptic about the rest (what it is, why he is here, what waits at the end).
4. **Warm up (light):** a fun, low-stakes would-you-rather (fly, or move through the world unseen) with nothing on the line, so the loop and the "how others chose" reveal are taught on a laugh before anything heavy lands.
5. **The turn:** the imp notes how painless that was, then puts something with weight in front of him.
6. **First heavy dilemma:** the clean classic trolley (five over one), the first real spike.
7. **The taunt:** the imp dismisses that as too easy and pivots to a harder one.
8. **Second dilemma:** the whose-life-counts trolley (see `gameplay.md`), where the clean headcount no longer settles it.
9. **Transition** into the blue/red button dilemma as the next beat.
10. **Breather (light):** after the button, the imp clears the air with a deliberately absurd would-you-rather (fight one duck the size of a horse, or a hundred horses the size of ducks), a laugh and a crowd split before the darkest beat, the imp giving the idiotic the same grave ceremony as the profound.
11. **The last bear.** A short imp hand-off, then the **man and the last bear** (see `gameplay.md`): the guide puts a gun in the dead judge's hands and makes him fire on one of two, needling him as ever about the life he spent sizing up everyone else, only now the other life on the scale is a whole species' future rather than one more person. It widens the questions to non-human life and to the generations not yet born.
12. **The closer.** A short sign-off: he has told the imp more than he thinks, and the imp is always here. The screen dissolves to black.

## Ending
Reflects the player's **profile** portrait (below): the game has **multiple endings**, and the kind of person the player turned out to be selects which one the imp delivers. The secret **shoot-the-imp** ending (see `gameplay.md`) is one of them. Replay value comes from answering differently for a different profile → a different ending and different narrator treatment. It is **not** a branching story: the path of questions is the same for everyone, only the ending differs.

**The money read (Chapter 3's close) is a portrait in miniature.** The money chapter closes on a short **read** (the cash-out shop that used to sit here was removed): after a finite pile of cash spent down across the offers, the imp reads what the player clung to, what they gave away too easily, and what they could not bring themselves to part with, and folds it silently into the profile. Cash, he notes, is the clearest window he has, it does not hide who a person is, it shows it. It points toward the ending without ever scoring, a small rehearsal of the final verdict-that-is-not-a-verdict.

## The profile
A silent **"who you are" portrait**, not a moral verdict. It tracks **what kind of person** the player is across a small set of **characterful axes where neither pole is the good one** — wider than ethics. First pass (aim for four to six, still open, see `open-questions.md`):
- Head vs. heart
- Self vs. others
- Order vs. chaos
- Risk vs. safety
- Honest vs. kind

The earlier **moral axes** — the one vs. the many, mercy vs. justice, ends vs. rules, order vs. freedom — **fold into this portrait as its ethics-flavoured part**, still used by the heavy dilemmas. Under the hood the axes map onto real leanings (utilitarian, deontological, cautious, candid, and so on), so the ending can paint a portrait — "you trusted your gut over your head, and you'd tell a hard truth before a kind lie" — **without ever scoring the player.**

**Both heavy and light questions can nudge these axes**, and a light would-you-rather can reveal as much as a trolley. But **not every question feeds the profile**: some are pure taste, kept only for the fun and the crowd stats (see `gameplay.md`), and that is fine.

**The requirement for any axis: neither pole is the "right answer."** That is why the axes describe *which way you lean*, not *how good you are*. The trap to avoid is a virtue axis where one pole simply reads as the good person: the rejected **selfish↔selfless** framing (selfless = good) smuggles a right answer back in and kills the point. Where an axis sounds close to that — self vs. others, say — it must stay descriptive (which the player centres in their reasoning), never selfish = bad.

The profile is tracked silently — it is **not** shown as a running gauge during play (see `art-and-ui.md`). The exact final list of axes, and which questions feed the profile versus stand as stats-only taste, are still open (see `open-questions.md`).
