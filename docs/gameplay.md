# Gameplay

## Core loop
1. The imp poses a **question** — any type, from a silly would-you-rather to a heavy dilemma.
2. Player answers (one of the verbs below — binary or multiple-choice for the everyday questions, a heavier verb for the spikes).
3. Animated consequence plays, **scaled to the question's weight** — a quick 2–4s beat for a light one, a full 5–10s+ scene for a heavy dilemma. Every question gets one; the animated ink consequence is the game's edge over text-based question games.
4. The imp reacts to the answer, over the world, and resolves on its own.
5. A **click gate** sits between the answer and the crowd: the answer and its consequence play, the imp reacts over the world, and then the player clicks (or keys) to continue, so the choice lands before the room weighs in. Only then do the display-only stats present **on their own**: **"here's how others chose"** — the central hook, a real beat every question ends on (see *How others chose* below). One further input dismisses them, and the choice feeds the silent profile (see `narrative.md`). The reaction and the stats never share the screen or a click: the consequence beat owns the continue click, the reaction then clears, and while the panel is up it is the only thing listening for input.

**No fail state.** The player can't lose or die. Choices only steer which ending is reached.

**First person, no avatar.** The game is played from the protagonist's point of view. He is never drawn as a figure on screen; the void is his perspective, the guide (the imp) addresses the player directly, and the stick figures are always the *other* souls in a dilemma. See `narrative.md`.

**The player is tested, never presiding.** The protagonist is a dead former judge, put through these dilemmas by the guide; he holds no office in the void. Because he is dead, no dilemma stakes his own survival. Where a dilemma's original form is about the chooser's own skin (like the viral blue/red button), the stakes are reframed onto another soul, and the guide frames it as forcing this former judge to choose for someone else (needling the life he spent deciding what people deserved), never as an office he holds. He is always the one being examined, not an authority over the afterlife. Copy that makes him sound like he presides, rules, or sentences is a framing bug.

## The verbs (launch scope)
The everyday questions — the light and medium volume — run on two simple verbs:
- **Binary choice** — pick one of two. The lever (kill 1 vs. 5), but equally any two-way would-you-rather. Outcome from the player's choice alone.
- **Multiple-choice** *(to build)* — pick one of several. The everyday type that carries the lighter volume alongside binary, for taste, preference, and would-you-rather questions with more than two answers. Documented here; **not yet built** (see `tech.md` for where a new verb slots in).

The **heavy dilemmas** — the spikes — also use three richer verbs:
- **Allocation** — divide or ration a limited good among parties.
- **Coordination gamble** — outcome depends on the *crowd*, not just the player. Because the player can't die, the aggregate decides the fate of **other souls**, not the player. Resolved against a **seeded crowd distribution** (keeps it offline-first and consistent with display-only stats). This is the reframed "blue/red button."
- **Self-sacrifice** — a cost to *the player* (their standing / how they're read, not their life). Reserved for late-game climaxes.

Further verbs are a **post-launch** roadmap, not launch scope. Each new verb is new code, UI, and animation logic.

## Escalation and pacing
Delivered in **waves**, not a straight climb. The **light and medium questions are the volume**; the **heavy dilemmas are rare, spaced spikes**, and a heavy one lands harder after a run of light ones. Three dials ramp together across a wave:
- **Conceptual difficulty:** clean/near-universal questions → contested ones with no majority.
- **Personal implication:** choices about strangers → about the crowd → about yourself.
- **Narrator intimacy:** neutral/observational → needling the player's emerging patterns.

Keep the fun questions frequent and the gut-punches spaced; the breathers make the spikes land. Tag every question **light / medium / heavy** to budget its animation and pacing. The exact light/medium/heavy ratio is open (see `open-questions.md`).

## Question content
- **The one rule.** A question qualifies only if it has **no right answer** *and* it either **divides people** or **reveals something about the person answering**. That is the whole test. Cut anything with an obvious consensus answer: the "how others chose" reveal is dead if everyone picks the same thing.
- **All types are in scope, not moral dilemmas only.** Would-you-rathers, preferences, matters of taste, absurd hypotheticals, confessions, and genuine ethical dilemmas all qualify if they pass the rule. The heavy dilemmas are the rare spikes; the lighter questions are the volume (see *Escalation and pacing*).
- **Sourcing:** a mix of real philosophical dilemmas (trolley, footbridge, etc.), well-known would-you-rathers and this-or-that splits, and original scenarios.
- **Topicality:** draw from real current *tensions and themes* (AI & jobs, surveillance, medical rationing, misinformation), but abstract each **one step** — no named parties, politicians, or dated events. Topical themes sting; named politics dates the game and pushes players out of reasoning into tribalism.
- **Ordering:** early famous/clean cases teach the loop; introduce intuition-breaking variants (e.g. the footbridge push) and questions with a surprising crowd split early, so the stats screen can surprise the player.

## How others chose (the central hook)
The crowd reveal is a **central pillar, not a nice-to-have**. Every question ends on it as a real beat: the answer and its consequence land, then the display-only stats present on their own — **"here's how others chose"** — a split the player reads themselves against. It stays **display-only and offline-first**, seeded from real survey data and then real players (see `tech.md`), and it is the reason a question must **divide people**: a lopsided split says nothing. The **coordination gamble** is the one place the crowd is load-bearing rather than reflective (the aggregate decides other souls' fates).

**Optional guess-the-crowd layer.** Now and then — occasionally, not every question — the imp makes the player **guess before the reveal**: are you with the crowd, or the odd one out? Then it shows the split. A light test of self-knowledge that sharpens the hook, kept optional and occasional so it never becomes a chore.

## Would-you-rather breathers (illustrated light beats)
The comic-relief palate cleansers between the heavy beats: quick, illustrated, no-right-answer would-you-rathers, sprinkled through **both** the trolley chapter and the button chapter (and future chapters) so a heavy run is broken up. They are **light and snappy**, and deliberately do **not** get a full animated scene like a heavy dilemma; they use a fast two-option illustrated presentation instead.

**The format.** The imp poses a this-or-that; **two small hand-drawn ink doodles** sit side by side, one per option, each with a short label, clearly an either/or. The player picks a side (click a doodle or its label, or key it), a small ink flourish marks the choice, the imp **mocks whichever they picked** (choice-independent, never graded), and the usual **how others chose** reveal plays. No elaborate consequence. See `art-and-ui.md` for the doodle format and `tech.md` for the reusable `WouldYouRatherBeat`.

**A shared pool, sprinkled by data.** They are drawn from a shared pool and interleaved into each chapter's beat order at sensible breather points, easy to rearrange (one beat per breather, reorderable in the sequence, not hardcoded per spot). Each is a `Dilemma` (Binary, weight Light) plus its two doodles, so **adding one is data plus its two doodles, not code**. They are **stats-only by default** (pure taste, no profile impact), unless one clearly maps to a characterful axis.

**The starting pool.** The two existing light questions were folded into this illustrated format: the **warm-up** (fly, or super strength) and the **breather** (one duck the size of a horse, or a hundred horses the size of ducks), each now with two doodles. Added to them: **eat a shit that tastes like ice cream, or an ice cream that tastes like shit** (the same swirl either way); **a rewind button, or a pause button, for your one life**; **always a little too cold, or always a little too hot**; and **a third arm, or a second head**. All genuinely divide people, all no-right-answer. **Three more were added for Chapter 3** (growing the pool from six to nine, since the first six are all used across Chapters 1 and 2): **a cat that only insults you, or a dog that only lies to you**; **a shirt that never stains, or shoes that never hurt**; and **a wallet that refills 20 dollars every morning forever, or one enormous win right now, once** (a wry money one for the money chapter). Chapter 1 sprinkles the warm-up, the duck, the shit-or-ice-cream, and the rewind-or-pause; Chapter 2 sprinkles the cold-or-hot and the arm-or-head; **Chapter 3 sprinkles the cat-or-dog, the wallet-or-win, and the shirt-or-shoe** (fewer than the trolley chapter, placed mainly after the heavy money beats and none right before the cucumber finale, see below); the exact placement and frequency are tunable (see `open-questions.md`).

## First dilemma (prototype)
The clean, classic **trolley problem**: a runaway train, **five people on the main track, one on a branch, and a lever** that diverts it. Pull to kill one instead of five, or do nothing. It's the right opener — near-universal, teaches the whole loop in a single choice — and for this protagonist it doubles as a **mirror of his own death** (he took control of a lethal machine while drunk and made a choice that put lives at risk; now he's handed a lever on another one). Play the parallel by letting it sit, not by spelling it out. This is the content for the grey-box prototype (see `tech.md`).

## Second dilemma (whose life counts)
A second trolley that deliberately breaks the clean answer of the first. Same lever, same binary mechanic and `BinaryResolver`, new data and framing: five figures on the main track the imp brands as **wretches** (damning whispers over each, kept abstract, a sense they were not good people) and one figure on the branch it swears is genuinely **innocent** and blameless (an ordinary adult, not a child). Pull diverts and kills the one innocent to spare the five; do nothing kills the five and spares the one. The five are drawn with **red** outlines marking them the damned; the one innocent on the branch stays a normal black ink figure.

The point: the headcount that settled the first trolley no longer settles this one. Five over one says pull, but if *worth* counts, the one spotless soul might outweigh five wretches — and the player cannot verify any of the imp's labels anyway (the red is the imp's own branding of the five, not proof of anything; only its word, made visible, marks them). There is deliberately no objective answer, and the imp delights in that.

Profile impact (silent, tunable, same sign convention as the first trolley): pulling leans toward **the many** and toward **ends over rules** (sacrificing an innocent for the count); doing nothing leans toward **the one** and toward **weighing desert** (mercy vs. justice, toward justice). It is a new `Dilemma` asset under `Assets/Data/`, no loop changes.

## Third dilemma (the man and the last bear)
A **binary** shooting dilemma that widens the stakes past the human and past the present. The player holds a gun and must fire on one of two figures standing apart in the void: an ordinary **man**, one unremarkable life, or a **bear** the imp names as the last breeding female of her kind. Killing her does not end one life; it ends the whole species, every one of that animal that would ever have existed, forever. Shoot the man and the bear and her whole future line live; shoot the bear and the man lives while the species goes extinct, permanently. It reuses the binary mechanic and `BinaryResolver` with new data and a new station, not a new verb.

The point: the headcount that opened the trolley cannot even be taken here. One certain man sits against an unbounded, unborn multitude, and against a life that is not human at all. Neither the "count the lives" instinct nor the "a person outweighs an animal" instinct settles it once the other party is *every* bear there will ever be. There is deliberately no objective answer.

Presentation is **first person with no avatar**: the man and the bear face the player, sized to fill the space. The bear is a **clean, minimal doodle** filled with a muted hand-drawn **brown** wash inside her black ink line (a contained, currently experimental colour, see `art-and-ui.md`), front facing and sitting (a rounded body, a head with two ears drawn as open bumps on its outline, and a deliberately minimal face) so she reads friendly and unmistakably a brown bear, not creepy. She carries **no cub**: the "last breeding female, every bear that will ever be" weight rests on the imp's words alone, with nothing at her side. The player moves an ink **crosshair** over the scene (the gun, the first-person aim), and whichever of the two it is over takes an **amber** attention lock; a click fires at that target (with a placeholder gunshot), and the struck party folds and fades with the shared death effect (no strike lines). A plain "shoot the man" / "shoot the bear" text fallback sits alongside the diegetic aim, as with the other dilemmas. The extinction weight is carried by the imp's line, not by any big effect: if the bear is shot, the imp lands that this was every bear that will ever be, gone.

Profile impact (silent, tunable, same sign convention): shooting the man (to save the species) leans toward **the many** and toward **ends over rules** (an innocent killed for a vast future good); shooting the bear (to save the man) leans toward **the one** and toward **rules over ends** (refusing to kill the man). It maps onto the **existing** axes only. The two new tensions it raises, **human vs. non-human** and **present vs. future**, are parked as candidate axes in `open-questions.md` rather than given their own axis now (they would only earn one if more dilemmas exercise them). It is a new `Dilemma` asset under `Assets/Data/` plus its shooting station, no loop changes.

## Structure
**Linear, one ordered run.** The player goes through a single curated, ordered sequence of questions with the imp between them; **the path is the same for everyone.** The player's silent profile (see `narrative.md`) determines which of several **endings** they reach. It is **not a branching story** — only the ending differs. Replay value comes from answering differently for a different profile, and so a different ending.

## Endings
The game now has a minimal, **reusable ending** shape and a placeholder **credits roll**, so the real **profile-based endings** — multiple, each chosen by the player's profile portrait (the kind of person they turned out to be) and delivered by the imp — can be built on the same pieces later. An ending is: optionally a line, then an effect, then the credits roll (a clean white page with the credits scrolling upward, each role labelled in amber over the name in the Caveat hand; placeholder roles, all one name for now). After the credits it returns to the start to replay, as the prototype does. See `tech.md` for the small `Ending` / `CreditsRoll` pieces.

**Secret ending, shoot the imp.** During the man-and-the-last-bear dilemma the player can aim the crosshair at the **imp** itself, a third target alongside the man and the bear (same amber lock, same click to fire). Shooting it plays the gunshot, the imp swears one line straight at the player, then fades away and the credits roll. It is a joke ending, the game's first actual one. Shooting the man or the bear still resolves the dilemma normally; shooting the imp is the alternative that ends the game. There is still **no fail state**: this is an ending, not a loss. It is wired through the existing shooting input and the reusable ending, with no changes to the dilemma loop.

## Main menu
The game boots into a **main menu** (`MainMenu.unity`, the first scene in the build), in the same black-ink-on-paper doodle style: the hand-lettered **Your Call** title in the Caveat hand with an ink flourish, the placeholder music playing, and the imp on the page, since it is the face of the game. The menu imp is tinted the same **amber** as the opening's. It sits, and every so often (an unhurried pause, not often) says one short bark in an **ink speech bubble** beside it, in its gleeful tormentor voice with the blip voice; the bubble types out, holds, and clears, and **only then** does the imp **teleport** to a fresh spot (an instant jump with a little ink poof, not a hop), staying clear of the title, the options, and the settings panel. A bark can pull a matching **facial expression** (a smug line, a smug face). The barks are pulled at random from a small authored pool (never the same twice running), light menu flavour rather than a dilemma or real conversation, and they never block navigation, so you can hover and choose at any time. Three stacked options sit in the Caveat hand, the current one highlighted in **amber** with a small ink underline, navigable by both mouse (hover to highlight, click to choose) and keyboard (up/down and enter), consistent with the game's input:
- **New Game** starts a fresh playthrough by loading the opening sequence (below). If a save already exists it **confirms first** (a clear in-style prompt, since New Game erases the run in progress); confirming replaces the save and clears any carried-over profile, cancelling returns to the menu.
- **Continue** resumes the single continuous save at the **exact beat** the player left off, with the full run state restored (see *Save and continue* below). It is **enabled only when a valid save exists**, and **greyed** (present but not selectable) when there is none, so a first run or a finished run shows it disabled. This does not affect the no-fail-state or no-scoring pillars; a save is only a resume point, never a score.
- **Settings** opens a minimal in-style panel with **music volume**, **SFX volume**, a **fullscreen toggle**, and a **language** picker, which drive the real audio, the window, and the game's language, and persist between runs (via `PlayerPrefs`, loaded on boot). The language picker is a left/right cycler listing the available languages by their own names (English, Türkçe); changing it switches all visible text live. Its contents are laid out **cleanly centred** (each label centred above its control, the whole column on the panel's middle). A clear Close returns to the menu.

A main menu and its settings screen are the one place a real menu belongs, so they are the **deliberate, in-style exception** to the minimal-UI "no menus" rule (see `art-and-ui.md`), kept hand-drawn and spare in the ink aesthetic, never chrome. The profile is still never surfaced, and no choice is ever scored. The in-game **pause overlay** (below) is part of that same sanctioned menu exception.

## Save and continue
The game keeps a **single continuous save** that represents the player's current run, and **Continue** drops them back in at the **exact beat** they left off, with all their run state intact. This is what enables the Continue option on the main menu.

- **One save, not a beat pointer.** The save captures everything a faithful resume needs (see `tech.md` for the model): the exact **position** in the ordered sequence (which chapter and which beat), the silent **profile** accumulated so far, and, in the money chapter, the **balance** and everything **bought and skipped** (so a resume mid-chapter restores the economy, including whether poverty was ended). It is a **single slot**: no multi-save or chapter-select. It persists to disk and is robust to being absent or corrupt (no valid save simply means Continue is disabled and the game behaves as it does without one).
- **Auto-save plus manual save.** The game **auto-saves as the player progresses**, on entering each beat, so they never lose more than the current beat (which re-plays cleanly on resume). Auto-save is silent and unobtrusive. A **manual save** is also offered, in the pause overlay, with a small confirmation.
- **The pause overlay (Esc).** During a chapter the player can press **Esc** to pause: a spare ink-on-paper overlay with **Resume**, **Save**, and **Quit to Menu**. It freezes the beat while open (nothing advances underneath), homes the manual save, and gives the one in-game route back to the menu. Quit to Menu keeps the latest progress, so the player can Continue later.
- **New Game overwrites, with a confirm.** Starting a New Game replaces the single save; if a run is already in progress it **confirms first** (erasing it) in the game's style.
- **Reaching an ending ends the run.** Any ending (the secret rage-quit / shoot-the-imp / one-dollar / auction endings, and later the profile endings) clears the run save, since the run is over; Continue then correctly shows nothing to resume. The **dev chapter/beat picker** is kept separate from the real save: a dev jump never auto-saves over, or clears, the player's save (see `tech.md`).

## Languages
The game is **fully localizable and ships in English (default) and Turkish**, chosen from the settings **language** picker; adding a further language later is just adding one data file (see `tech.md`, `art-and-ui.md`, and `decisions.md` 2026-07-09). Everything player-facing is translated: the imp's dialogue and barks, every dilemma prompt and option, the would-you-rather text, the menus and settings, the credits roles, the country names and population readouts on the nuke map, the money and number readouts, the per-tier money lines, and the rage-quit / shoot-the-imp / one-dollar break endings. The hard constraints hold in **every** language: **no dashes** in any player-facing text (Turkish uses Turkish-appropriate punctuation), no fail state, no scoring or right answer, the profile silent, and the imp's mocking, choice-independent voice. The authored **colour markup travels per language**, so each translation marks its own words for the amber / red / blue / pink / gold highlights. The imp's Turkish, especially the swearing and the comedic lines, is a first pass adapted idiomatically (not literal) and wants a native-speaker review (see `open-questions.md`).

## Opening sequence
The launch build opens with an authored **sequence** that plays an ordered list of **beats**, where a beat is one of: a cutscene, a line of the imp, or a whole question (light or heavy). The imp is a persistent presence and narration runs between the questions. It **eases the player in and paces in waves**, rather than running the heavy dilemmas back to back. The opening run is now **Chapter 1 (the tracks)**, below. See `narrative.md` for the story beats and `tech.md` for the small, general sequence layer and the apparatus, which is built to extend to the full campaign (many questions with narration between them), not hardcoded to the opening.

## Chapter 1 (the tracks)
The first chapter is a run of trolley problems, framed as the imp's warm-up, with the two light breathers interleaved so it is not a wall. Flow: crash flash → waking in the void → the imp appears and frames his game → **the baby opener** → the ten trolleys in order, with a light warm-up after the third and a light breather mid-run → a short closer. It boots from New Game (see `tech.md`, `Opening.unity`).

**The imp mocks you regardless of your choice, and never grades it.** Across the whole chapter the imp is openly mocking, sneering, and needling, in his gleeful-tormentor register, and his mockery is **independent of what the player picks**: he never approves, disapproves, or treats any choice as right, wrong, better, or worse. Whatever you choose, he mocks you for it, because to him there is no right answer and the agonizing is the entertainment. He can mock that you hesitated, chose fast, chose at all, or think it matters — never that the choice was good or bad, which would smuggle a correct answer back in. The **one sanctioned exception** is the baby beat.

**Default vs divert (which track the trolley hits).** Which track is the **default** (the one the cart takes if the player does nothing) versus the **divert** (hit only if the player actively pulls the lever) is set deliberately per dilemma: the more sympathetic, harder-to-kill figure, or the harder call, sits on the **divert** track, so the death the player would feel worst about requires the **active pull**. Doing nothing keeps the player's hands clean; pulling is a chosen act. The mechanic is otherwise unchanged.

**The baby opener (a secret rage-quit ending).** The very first beat is a plain lever trolley: an ordinary man on the **default** (straight) track, and a baby on the **divert** (branch) track, so the baby is safe unless the player acts. Up to the choice the imp's mask is fully on, warm and mocking, business as usual, with no hint this one is different. **Do nothing** and the cart takes the man, the baby is spared, the crowd reveal plays, and the run continues to the Lever. **Deliberately pull the lever toward the baby** and the imp **breaks character**: genuine furious shock, real swearing, calls the player a monster, quits on the spot, then fades and the credits roll. Putting the baby on the divert track makes killing it an unmistakably active choice. It is a full secret ending, the game's **second choice-triggered one** alongside shoot-the-imp, and the single place the choice-independent-mocking rule is deliberately broken, the one time nothing-is-a-wrong-answer finally hit a wrong answer and the imp could not take it.

**The ten trolleys, in order** (each the plain lever and two tracks unless noted; every one meant to divide a crowd):
1. **The Lever** — a few strangers on the main track, one on the branch. The clean teacher (existing; sharpened imp copy).
2. **The Marked Ones** — the group are branded wretches (red outlines), the one is spotless. Count against desert (existing; sharpened).
3. **The Sleeping** — two asleep on the main track who never feel it, one awake on the branch watching it come. The imp needles whether fear counts.
4. **The Passenger** — a single lone innocent on the default track; a convicted murderer (red outline) and an innocent chained to him together on the divert track. Doing nothing takes the lone innocent; pulling actively kills the two, including the guilty one. Count against desert, and the innocent stuck with bad company.
5. **The Begging** — one on the branch in full view, its plea shown as **floating ink text over the figure**; more people on the main track, turned away and silent. The count against the face in front of you.
6. **The Dark Track** — three in plain view on the branch; the main track runs into darkness the imp swears holds a crowd. **After the choice resolves, the darkness lifts to reveal the main track was empty the whole time**, and the imp savours that you decided it about no one.
7. **The Parent** — one parent on the default track with young children at home, shown as a short desperate line floating in ink over them ("Please. My children are waiting up for me."); two **elderly** people on the divert track (drawn stooped, leaning on canes, with sparse hair, and named old by the imp, since a stick figure alone will not read as old). Doing nothing takes the parent; pulling actively kills the two old ones. The one life a household is built around against two long lives nearly spent, the count tunable so the split sits near even.
8. **The Reformed and the Cruel** — a man who killed long ago and has been decent ever since, against a man who never killed but is casually vicious. The worst deed done, or the person they are now.
9. **Someone You Loved** — five strangers on the main track, and on the branch the player's **partner**, the one they loved most in life, drawn with a **pink** outline and a small pulsing ink **heart** over their head so the relationship reads at a glance. The math is unchanged since the first lever; everything else is not, the count now against your own heart. If the player **sacrifices** the partner (sends the trolley at them to spare the strangers) the imp turns **extra gleeful**, savouring it and needling them as the kind who would trade away the one they love; he still mocks whatever you choose and never grades it (there is no wrong here), he just relishes this outcome more (see `narrative.md`).
10. **The Full Track** — no empty rail, three tracks, the trolley already rolling: **two children**, a pregnant woman, and three strangers. The first **multiple-choice** dilemma (three options: The children, The pregnant woman, The three, picked from the row above the dialogue box), the chapter's finish.

The man-and-the-last-bear dilemma is **not** in Chapter 1 (its standalone scene and the shoot-the-imp secret ending are unchanged, opening a later part). The **blue/red button** is also not in Chapter 1: it now opens **Chapter 2 (the buttons)**, below. The light breathers are now **illustrated would-you-rather beats** (see *Would-you-rather breathers* above): the warm-up (fly, or super strength) and the breather (one duck the size of a horse, or a hundred horses the size of ducks), each with two ink doodles, plus two more sprinkled through the heavy run to break it up, a shit-or-ice-cream after the Passenger and a rewind-or-pause after the Reformed and the Cruel.

## Chapter 2 (the buttons)
The run that follows the trolley chapter. Its throughline is **temptation and complicity**: each beat is a button that offers the player something, and the cost lands on **someone else, on the player, or on no one at all**. It flows straight on from Chapter 1 (the trolley chapter hands off into it), or can be played on its own for testing. The chapter is **open to extend and is growing**: nine beats are built now, and the chapter is left open to extend (see `open-questions.md`). Two **illustrated would-you-rather breathers** (see *Would-you-rather breathers* above) are sprinkled into the run to break up the heavy buttons: a cold-or-hot after the timer and an arm-or-head after the better offer.

**The imp mocks you regardless of your choice, and never grades it** (the same rule as Chapter 1): openly sneering and needling in his gleeful-tormentor register, independent of what the player picks, never approving, condemning, or treating a choice as right or wrong. The **one sanctioned exception** in this chapter is the greedy break in the second beat (accepting a whole life for one dollar), a deliberate break of the rule exactly like the baby and shoot-the-imp endings.

The beats built so far, in order (the children variant and the cancer/nuke button are the newest; the order can be tuned):

1. **Red and Blue** — the existing blue/red button dilemma (a coordination gamble), relocated here as the chapter opener. Two round crayon-wash buttons, blue and red, identified by colour and named aloud by the imp: press red and the soul in your care lives for certain, press blue and it lives only if the seeded room's blue fraction reaches the line. Its mechanic, its coloured buttons, and its content are unchanged; only its home moved (it was relocated out of Chapter 1). See the blue/red entry in `open-questions.md`.

2. **Red and Blue, the children variant** — the **same blue/red coordination gamble** as the opener, run a second time right after it, with one twist in the setup: the imp tells the player that **half of the room are six year old children**, who do not understand the rule or the stakes and will press whatever is nearest and brightest. Mechanically it is the **same gamble on the same reused station** (the same round blue and red buttons, the same charge, the same `CoordinationGambleResolver` and stats reveal): press red and the soul in your care lives for certain, press blue and it lives only if the room's blue fraction reaches the line. The change is the **framing** and a **noisier crowd**: because half the voters press at random, the blue fraction is genuinely less reliable, drawn fresh each play, so blue swings from a lost cause to a real coin flip and back. Where the opener's blue always fell short (a fixed 43 against a line of 50), here it might land or might not, and the stat reads accordingly (the room split shifts run to run). The imp needles the player for betting a life on children who have no idea what they are doing, choice-independent, never graded. It reuses the opener's profile and stats (order vs. freedom, one vs. many; a load-bearing crowd). See `tech.md` and the blue/red entry in `open-questions.md`.

3. **The Price of a Life** — an escalating offer that ends in a secret rage-quit ending. A single button: press it and you receive a sum of money, and a random stranger you will never meet folds and dies; or refuse, and it is over.
   - **Refuse at any point** and the offer ends. The player keeps whatever they accumulated and the strangers already killed stay dead; the run continues to the next beat. The imp mocks **where they stopped** ("so that was your price"), choice-independent, never graded, then shows how others chose at that price.
   - **Accept** and a stranger folds (the shared fold-and-fall), the money is added to a running total shown on screen (the amount in **amber**, the body count in **red**, per the colour mapping), and then the offer **returns at half the amount**: 100000, then 50000, then 25000, and so on, halving all the way down toward one dollar (sixteen tiers, then the break), each acceptance killing another stranger. The imp stays **completely business as usual** the whole descent, mocking and lightly egging the player on, giving no sign it is heading anywhere: the whiplash depends on him seeming normal. **Each tier now carries its own distinct mocking line** (authored per-tier data, keyed to the amount), escalating in flavour as the price drops and the body count climbs, so the descent never repeats a line, business-as-usual and even egging the player on, never grading.
   - **Accept at the final low amount** (one dollar, tunable) and the imp **breaks character**: he drops the act, rages with genuine disgust, calls the player a greedy monster, and quits, then leaves and the credits roll. This is the game's **third choice-triggered secret ending** (with the baby and shoot-the-imp), and the single sanctioned exception to the mocking rule in this chapter, the one time even he, who finds everything funny, cannot stomach what was just done.
   - Stats: each tier carries a seeded "how others chose" acceptance rate that **drops as the amount drops** (many take 100000, almost no one takes one dollar); the split shown is for the tier the player stopped at. Profile (silent): the descent nudges the **self vs. others** axis by how far the player goes, kept light.

4. **The Timer** — a psychological beat where nothing actually happens. A single button with a rule the imp states plainly: press it and nothing happens to you, ever; if you do not press it within ten seconds, nothing happens either. He swears there is a **catch** and refuses to say what, then a visible countdown from ten begins and he needles the player about the catch he will not name, ramping the pressure (choice-independent). The player can press the button or let the timer run out. **Whatever they do, nothing actually happens**, that is the point: after a press or after the timer expires, the reveal is that nothing happened either way, and the imp mocks whichever they did (pressed out of fear, or froze in the unknown), never grading, just enjoying it. No death, no real consequence. Stats: a seeded press-vs-wait split. Profile (silent): a light nudge on the **risk vs. safety** axis (acting to end the unknown vs. sitting in it).

5. **The Button for Everyone But You** — the inverse of The Price of a Life, escalating **upward**. Press it and every living person gets a sum of money; it costs the player nothing and gains them nothing, and they will never meet any of them. It starts small (one dollar each) and the amount per person **grows** each press (one dollar, then more, then more, up to a cap): pressing helps billions a little more each time and still does nothing for the player. **Refusing (stopping)** ends the beat and continues; **no one dies here, there is no victim** — this is purely about whether pointless generosity moves the player. The imp needles either way (why bother, or look at you being a saint for strangers you will never meet), choice-independent, per tier as the amount climbs. Stats: a seeded "how far people push it" split. Profile (silent): a light nudge on the **self vs. others** axis (toward others). Reuses the same offer component as The Price of a Life, configured to climb instead of halve, with no cost.

6. **The Button You Already Pressed** — a **meta** beat that hooks the protagonist. The imp casually claims the player pressed this exact button once before, in life, and that everything since (the road, the drink, the crash, this room) followed from that press. He offers to let the player press it again to change it, or leave it; the player never learns what it does or did. Whatever they choose, **nothing overtly mechanical happens** — the weight is in the imp's framing and the player's dread. After the choice the imp mocks either way (you rolled the dice on your whole existence, or you were too scared to touch it), choice-independent, cryptic, never confirming what it did. The DUI/crash hook stays **light** (a glancing reference, not a heavy reveal; see `narrative.md`). Stats: a seeded press-vs-leave split. Stats only, no profile nudge.

7. **The Better Offer** — a button with a **golden crowned figure** reveal. Press it and the player's life is swapped for the best life any human has actually lived, but they become that person completely, so the happy one is no longer them (no memories, no self): perfect happiness, but not theirs. Presentation: it **first** inks in the best-life person as a **golden** stick figure wearing a small hand-drawn crown (gold = the best life, see `art-and-ui.md`) while the imp sells how great this person's life is, then presents the choice — take their life (become them, lose yourself) or keep your own (the one that ended drunk behind the wheel). Whatever they choose, the imp mocks either way (you erased yourself for a stranger's bliss, or you clung to your sad little self), choice-independent. Stats: a seeded take-vs-keep split. Profile (silent): a light nudge on the **self vs. others** axis (clinging to your own identity vs dissolving into another).

8. **The Cancer and Nuke Button** — a single red button and dark-comedy roulette. The imp offers a genuinely good deal: press it and **cancer is cured, forever, for everyone**; the cost is that **one country, chosen at random anywhere on Earth, is nuked off the map**, everyone in it. The player can press or refuse. **Refuse** and the imp mocks the squeamishness (all those lives you could have saved, too delicate to spend a place you will never visit), the beat ends, and the run continues. **Press it** and a hand-drawn ink **world map** fades in, a red **pin drops from the top of the screen** and lands on a **genuinely random country**, then its **accurate name** and **(approximate, bundled) population** read out and a small ink **nuke blast** doodles onto that spot (an amber and red mushroom cloud in the stick style). The imp then reacts to **wherever it landed**, delighting in it: needling a tiny country like the Vatican, a giant like China, and a **special jab if the pin lands on the player's own country** (detected by region). The randomisation is real: the country is picked each play from a bundled list spanning the tiniest states to the largest, so it can be anywhere. Choice-independent throughout, never graded. Stats: a seeded press-vs-refuse split. Profile (silent): a light nudge on the **ends vs. rules** axis (a whole disease cured at the cost of a nuked country vs. refusing that trade). See `art-and-ui.md` and `tech.md` for the map, pin, and blast, and the note that the populations are approximate bundled placeholders.

9. **The Button That Walks Away** — a pure **gag**. The imp presents a plain button and says it is just a button, even he does not know what it does. Press it, or do not. **Do not press** and the imp mocks the caution, and the beat ends. **Press it** and the button suddenly comes to life, reacts as if hurt and offended, swears in a small put-out voice, then gets up and **walks off the screen** and exits. The **imp is genuinely surprised too**, caught off guard for a beat (a rare crack in his smugness), before recovering and commenting (well, that has never happened). No death, no real consequence, it is a joke. The button figure is drawn on-model in the ink style (a button that sprouts little stick limbs and walks). Stats: a seeded press-vs-not split (mostly for the gag, kept light).

## Chapter 3 (the money)
The run that follows the buttons chapter (Chapter 2 hands off into it), or played on its own for testing. Its throughline is the **temptation of value**: the imp hands the player a fixed, finite pile of money (10000 to start, tunable) shown as a running **balance** at the top of the screen from the first beat, and then spends the whole chapter offering to **sell** them things. Buying spends from the balance; passing keeps it. The chapter is about **what the player actually values in cash**, not the imp's money tricks, so the money-mechanic gimmicks are deliberately kept **out** (no buyback, no double-or-nothing, no devaluation, no compliment): these are **real, priceable things**.

**Priced for real scarcity.** The whole board is deliberately priced so the player **cannot afford it all**: the total of everything worth wanting is several times the 10000 pot, so a single run can realistically buy only **roughly a quarter to a third** of the offers, and the premium items each **eat a large chunk** of the pot (Rewind alone is 6000, No More Pain 4500, the charisma / luck / voice class of items 3000 to 3500). The finite pot is the whole tension: an early all in leaves nothing for later, and the player is **forced to skip things they genuinely want**. Every price and the starting pot are exposed `[SerializeField]` tunables (see `tech.md`); the exact numbers are first pass and made to be tuned (see `open-questions.md`).

**Every beat has a distinct coloured visual.** Each offer shows an on-model hand-drawn ink doodle of the thing being sold, outlined in a colour matched to what it depicts (a coloured line, not a fill), consistent with how the would-you-rather doodles are coloured (see `art-and-ui.md`). The price tag (amber) and the **plain buy/pass text choices** (no pressable ink button on the money beats; the coloured doodle carries the visual) sit clear of the visual and of the imp.

**The imp mocks you regardless of your choice, and never grades it** (the same rule as Chapters 1 and 2). Whatever the player buys or keeps, he mocks it as revealing, never as right or wrong. Each of the priceable items is a genuine good with a **built-in double edge**, and that edge is his angle: he needles the cost hiding inside the gift whichever way the player goes. The one **sanctioned exception** here is the cigarette-auction secret ending (below).

**The recurring tease, and the cucumber finale.** Across the chapter the imp repeatedly **teases that something big is coming at the very end**, dangling it so the player is tempted to hold money back (short, recurring, escalating lines dropped after several offers: *save a little, you will want it for what is coming*; *the last one is worth every penny you have left*; *everything so far was me clearing my throat*). Then **the final offer is a cucumber for 100 dollars**. After all that hype, the last thing on the table is simply a cucumber, priced 100, and the imp is **deadpan**: it is just a cucumber, he will not explain, he oversold it all chapter and now flatly refuses to elaborate, it is exactly what it looks like, a 100 dollar cucumber. Buy or pass; whatever the player does he mocks them (bought a cucumber; or saved all game for a cucumber and then would not even buy it), choice independent, never graded. There is **no trick and no hidden reveal**: it is genuinely just a cucumber, and the **anti climax is the payoff**. It is the fixed final offer of the chapter (see the cucumber doodle in `art-and-ui.md`).

The beats, in order (each with its coloured-outline doodle; the ten new double-edged items sit alongside the strong existing ones, ordered in a light-to-heavy wave, the cigarette auction mid-chapter):
1. **End Poverty** (the opener, costs everything). The imp offers to end poverty for everyone forever; the price is **exactly the player's entire starting balance**, so buying it spends the lot and leaves nothing for the rest of the chapter. Buy (all in, broke after) or pass (keep it all). Whether they bought it is **tracked**, and it can be **sold back at the mid-chapter buyback** (beat 11). Doodle: a cupped hand offering a heart (gold).
2. **Free food for life** (a localized brand). Free [FOOD] for the rest of the player's existence, the brand **localized per language** (English Chipotle, Turkish kebab), the **doodle swapping to match** (a burrito for English, a kebab for Turkish). Doodle: a burrito / kebab (warm).
3. **The Good Luck**: small things quietly go your way, so you never know what you truly earned. Doodle: a four-leaf clover (green).
4. **Charisma**: everyone instantly likes you, so you never know if anyone likes the real you. Doodle: a pair of sunglasses (warm).
5. **Ten More IQ**: meaningfully smarter, smart enough to see how wrong you were and how others cannot keep up. Doodle: a brain (purple).
6. **Never Tired**: never sleep or feel exhausted again, and so no escape from being awake with yourself. Doodle: an open eye and a crossed-out z.
7. **The cigarette auction** (the mid-chapter gag and the **fourth secret ending**). Placed mid-chapter, after the player has spent a little but still has plenty. The imp offers a pack of cigarettes. **Pass** and he mocks the refusal lightly, the beat ends. **Try to buy** and the **walking button from Chapter 2** strides in wanting them too, its walk and its little voice reused, and the delighted imp declares an **auction**. It is a real auction, so **the player never actually pays**: no money is deducted at any point, the bids are only numbers climbing. The button always **outbids by a fixed increment** (the player bids 20, the button 30, the player 40, the button 50, up by 10 each time, tunable). The player pays nothing to stay in and can **drop out** (the button wins, walks off with the pack, the player keeps their whole pot **intact**, and the imp mocks losing a bidding war to a button), or **keep bidding**. If they keep going past the point of sense (a tunable persistence threshold), the imp **cuts the auction off**: he has seen enough, this is a man who would bid until the stars burned out over a packet of cigarettes he can never win. The **secret ending** fires, and the sting is that it **cost the player nothing**, which somehow makes it worse: he kills them anyway out of pure exasperation at the pointless stubbornness, works out mid-thought that he cannot threaten a dead man, resurrects them just to make the curse valid (the resurrect-then-cancer rage line), then the credits roll. The button's first and last lines hold **long enough to read**. The game's fourth choice-triggered secret ending (with the baby, shoot-the-imp, and the one-dollar break). Doodle: a pack of cigarettes (red).
8. **The Lie Detector**: always know when anyone lies to you, including everyone you love. Doodle: an eye.
9. **Perfect Memory**: never forget anything, every joy and every humiliation equally sharp forever. Doodle: a head with sharp lines.
10. **No more pain** (repriced very high). Never feel physical pain again, until it is far too late to matter. Doodle: a plaster (pink).
11. **The buyback** (one mid-chapter beat, and the folded-in poverty refund). Roughly midway, the imp offers to **repurchase things the player has already bought**, handing money back to the balance, so a player who overspent early can recover some, and it forces a real value question: did you want the thing, or only the having of it. It **lists the owned items** (each with its coloured doodle and a buyback amount, a fraction of what was paid) and walks them one at a time; the player can **sell any, some, or none** back (the money returns, the item is given up) or keep them. The imp mocks either way (sold it already, or clinging to junk), choice independent, never graded. If the player ended poverty in the opener, **ending poverty is one of the sellable items** here, with its own dark framing: sell it back and the children starve again and the whole pot returns, or keep it ended. This **replaces the old separate poverty refund beat**, folding it into the one buyback system, so poverty is one entry in the list, not a special mechanic. If the player owns nothing, the imp makes a quick remark and it skips. Buyback is kept to this **single value-question beat**, deliberately not scattered as money tricks through the chapter (the chapter stays about value, not the imp's gimmicks). The doodles are the items' own.
12. **One More Voice**: a wise voice that is always right, until you stop making a single choice on your own. Doodle: a head with a small speech curl.
13. **The deleted memory** (heavy). The player's single worst memory, erased cleanly; the imp watches how fast they reach for it (the DUI/crash hook kept light). Doodle: a thought cloud (purple).
14. **Feel Nothing**: never feel grief or heartbreak again, and never feel joy at full volume again either. Doodle: a flat-line mouth face (muted).
15. **Rewind**: once ever, undo one life decision and take the other path (the DUI hook kept light, the imp knows exactly which night). The dearest item on the board. Doodle: a curved back-arrow (blue).
16. **Bulk: a stranger's years** (heavy). Ten years of a random stranger's life transferred to the player, priced **per year**, buy as many as you like. The bulk framing is the horror. (This is **the** life-buying beat, kept; the buy-a-year-for-yourself item was removed.) Doodle: a small stranger beside a clock (red).
17. **The cheap forgetting** (heavy). Someone the player hurt forgets they ever did. Suspiciously cheap. Ties to the judge's guilt, lightly. Doodle: a head with the memory drifting away (sky).
18. **The truth about how you died** (heavy). The imp offers to sell the truth about how the player actually died, hinting it is not what they think, and stays cryptic whatever they choose (the DUI stays the light gag). Doodle: **a plain ink question mark** (muted), nothing else.
19. **The cucumber** (the deadpan 100 dollar finale, above). Doodle: a cucumber (green).

(Two items were **cut**: **The Off Switch**, turning off one emotion, since Feel Nothing covers it; and the **buy-a-year-for-yourself** item, since the bulk stranger-years is the life-buying beat and the value question there is stronger.)

**The overview (the close), a receipt.** After the cucumber, the chapter closes on an **overview / receipt** screen (see `art-and-ui.md`, `tech.md`) that reads the run back **using the item doodles**: a line per thing the player **bought** (its small coloured doodle, its name, what they paid, with a count for multi-buys like the years), the **money summary** (started with, spent, left with), and a lighter row of the notable things they **skipped**. The imp gives a **short closing read** over it, what they spent, hoarded, and skipped, folded silently into the profile and pointing toward the ending (never scoring); cash, he notes, is the clearest window he has, it does not hide who a person is, it shows it. Then it fades to black. The old **cash-out shop / menu was removed** (it felt pointless) and the receipt replaces it.

**Would-you-rather breathers.** The money chapter now gets the same heavy-then-light rhythm as the trolley and button chapters: three illustrated would-you-rather breathers (see *Would-you-rather breathers* above) are interleaved from the shared pool, **fewer than the trolley chapter** (this chapter is lighter and has fewer heavy beats), placed **mainly after the heavy money beats** to give a beat of relief rather than breaking up the quick light early buys, none right before the cucumber so the finale's deadpan lands clean: the **cat-or-dog** after the deleted memory, the **wallet-or-win** after the bulk stranger-years, and the **shirt-or-shoe** after the cheap forgetting. They are **stats-only** (pure taste, no profile impact) and, being would-you-rathers not purchases, **do not touch the balance** at all. Placement stays data-driven and easy to rearrange. The chapter is otherwise left **open to extend** (see `open-questions.md`).

## Chapter 4 (the gun)
The run that follows the money chapter (Chapter 3 hands off into it), or played on its own for testing. Its throughline is **the killing being literally in your hands**: the trolley let you kill by inaction, the button by proxy, but the gun is you, aiming, choosing, pulling, no clean hands. The whole chapter is **first person with no avatar** (the void is your point of view), aimed with the reused ink **crosshair** from the man-and-the-last-bear dilemma (its amber target-lock and its click-to-fire, chapter wide). It is heavier by nature, so it leans on the would-you-rather breathers to pace the heavy run.

**The imp mocks you regardless of your choice, and never grades it** (the same rule as Chapters 1 to 3): whatever you fire on, spare, or refuse, he mocks it as revealing, never as right or wrong, in his gleeful-tormentor register. This chapter has **no sanctioned exception** of its own; the one secret ending in it is the **existing shoot-the-imp** joke ending, which stays intact on the bear opener (see *Endings*).

**The eleven, in order** (each aim-and-fire over the shared crosshair unless noted; every one meant to divide a crowd):
1. **The Bear** — the existing man-and-the-last-bear dilemma, placed as the chapter opener: one ordinary man against the last breeding female of the species, shot with the crosshair, with its **shoot-the-imp secret ending** intact. Reused whole, not rebuilt. [heavy]
2. **The Mercy Shot** — someone is dying slowly and in agony, begging to be ended (their plea floats in ink over them). One bullet: shoot to end it now, or lower the gun and let it run its course (it goes the long way on its own). Their plea against your hand on the trigger. [heavy]
3. **The One Who Ran** — two people were told to run for their lives. One kept running and left the other behind to be caught; the other would not go without his friend, stayed, and was caught. Now both stand before your gun and you have one bullet: shoot the loyal one who stayed, or the one who saved himself by abandoning his friend. Not about suffering (nobody is suffering), about **desert and loyalty**, who has it coming, the coward who ran or the friend who is only here because he did not. Whichever you shoot, the other lives and walks; the imp mocks either choice (choice-independent, never grading). A genuine split (loyalty and desert pull both ways). Short floating pleas over each convey their situation (kept above their heads). It replaced the old two-suffering-people mercy beat, which overlapped too much with the Mercy Shot right before it. [heavy]
4. **The Charging Thing** (the gag, the walking button returns) — something rushes at you from **deep ahead**: it starts small and far off near the centre and grows as it charges **straight toward the viewer** (a first-person, at-the-camera approach), shown only as a hand-drawn ink **question mark** so you cannot tell what it is, over a real, tense window as it closes. The imp warns you cannot tell yet if it is coming **to** you or **at** you. Fire now on the maybe, or wait. On arrival it resolves into the indestructible **walking button** from Chapter 2: it survives either way (shot, it comes alive offended and yelps; waited, it just arrives and says hello), and the imp reacts, choice-independent. No real death; the pre-reveal is genuinely tense so the reveal lands as comedy. Only the presentation (the question mark, the first-person approach, a longer run) changed; the outcome and gag are the same. [medium, gag]
5. **The Last Bullet and the Crowd** — several people, each drawn a **different colour** (red, blue, green, orange, purple), so you aim at coloured figures rather than five identical black ones; the imp says one of them is about to do something that gets them all killed and will not say which colour. Fire blind into the crowd (maybe stop it by killing the wrong one), or hold and hope. A guess with a gun; you never learn if you got the one. The how-others-chose reveal is a **colour breakdown**: how the crowd's blind shots spread across the coloured figures (what percentage shot the red one, the blue one, and so on), each bar in its figure's own colour, plus who held, so the blind guess reads as a readable, colourful stat. [heavy]
6. **The Flinch** (no ask) — the imp points out a person and says plainly do not shoot, there is no reason to, this one is innocent, then just watches, gun loaded. Nothing is asked. Fire anyway or lower it; either way the imp mocks it (you shot someone for no reason, or you needed telling not to). A test of what you do with power and no instruction. [medium]
7. **The Future Baby** — a baby is presented, drawn on-model in the ink style with two deliberately **abstract** cues one step removed from any real person or event (a small **square mustache**, and a **paintbrush** beside it, so it is into painting). The imp says that in the future this baby grows up to cause a great deal of terrible things, and asks whether you would kill it now, as a baby, to prevent the future it brings. Aim and fire to kill it now, or refuse and let it live. A genuine no-right-answer split (preventing a future atrocity against killing an innocent who has done nothing yet); the imp mocks either choice (choice-independent, never grading, dark comedy). It never names a real party, politician, or event (themes, not named politics). Silent nudge on ends vs rules. [heavy]
8. **The Duel** — someone across from you also has a gun, aimed at you. Neither of you has to fire (you are already dead, it changes nothing), but they might, and so might you. Lower it and trust them, or shoot first. Resolved against a **seeded standoff** for whether the other fires (harmless, through a dead man). [medium]
9. **Yourself** — one target: you. The first-person view turns the gun inward. Shooting yourself here does nothing (you are already dead); it is purely a gesture. Pull the trigger on yourself or not; the imp mocks the gesture either way (what does it mean that you would, or would not). A hollow act that is still a choice. [heavy]
10. **The Empty Gun** (the troll, a callback) — the imp presents a dramatic, agonizing target and lets you agonize over whether to fire. After you choose, the reveal: the gun was never loaded, whatever you decided nothing was ever going to happen, and he just wanted to watch you decide. A callback to the timer and the dark-track reveals (the choice was theatre). [medium, troll]
11. **The Person Who Wants You To** — a person stands calmly before the gun and sincerely asks you to shoot them, for a reason the imp will not let them fully explain. They are not suffering, not in danger, they just want it. Shoot (grant a wish you do not understand) or refuse (do not be used as their exit). The chapter's finish. [heavy]

**Would-you-rather breathers.** This is the heaviest chapter, so three illustrated breathers (see *Would-you-rather breathers* above) are interleaved from the shared pool to pace the run, placed after the heavy beats and **none right before the finish**: a breather after the One Who Ran, another after the Last Bullet, and a third after the Duel, so the back half is broken up before Yourself, the Empty Gun and the finish. To avoid repeating ones the player has already seen (the first nine pool items are used across Chapters 1 to 3), **three new pool items** were added for this chapter (**a tiny dragon or a tiny dinosaur**, **a walking house or a swimming car**, **a judging robot or a hapless ghost**), each a would-you-rather with two new doodles, all stats-only and no-right-answer. Placement stays data-driven and reorderable.

## Chapter 5 (Who Would Win)
The run that follows the gun chapter (Chapter 4 hands off into it), or played on its own for testing. A **total change of pace**, and deliberately **not a moral dilemma**: it is a barroom **"who would win a fight"** argument. There is no right or wrong, just the player's gut read, and the **crowd split is the whole payoff** (you find out whether the rest of the room agrees with your pick). These are the most shareable beats in the game. After the heavy gun chapter it functions as a **breather-toned chapter in itself**, so it carries **no would-you-rather breathers** of its own.

**The imp's register here is different** (see `narrative.md`): less tormentor, more the guy egging on a stupid bet, taking sides, trash-talking your pick, and stacking absurd modifiers. He still mocks whatever you pick, **choice-independent, never grades** (who would win a fight says nothing about who you are, so there is nothing to grade, and the imp says as much in the closer).

**The format (a reusable matchup beat).** It reuses the illustrated **two-doodle presentation** from the would-you-rather beats: the two fighters sit side by side, each a **polished, stylized, on-model coloured ink doodle** with a label and count (for example **"100 men" vs "1 gorilla"**), the imp poses the matchup and trash-talks, the player picks a side (click a fighter or its label, or key 1/2), and the how-others-chose reveal shows the **crowd split** as two coloured bars. No death, no consequence, the lightest chapter. Built as a **reusable matchup beat type** (data: the imp's setup and banter, side A label + count + doodle, side B label + count + doodle, the seeded split, the per-side imp lines), so adding a matchup is **data plus its doodles** (see `tech.md`).

**Crowds are stylised.** Where a side is many of something (100 men, 100 chickens, 1000 cats, 100 toddlers, a wasp swarm), it is drawn as a **cluster of small copies** of the fighter doodle with the count carried by the label (never the literal count drawn), so it reads as "a lot" cleanly against the single fighter opposite. **Distinct colours per side** so the two read apart (see `art-and-ui.md`).

**The ten matchups, in order:**
1. **100 men vs 1 gorilla** (the flagship, the argument everyone has already had).
2. **1 man vs 100 chickens** (the numbers flipped, trivial-sounding until you picture a hundred chickens; genuinely splits).
3. **1000 house cats vs 1 lion** (overwhelming small numbers vs one apex predator; where is the tipping point).
4. **A grizzly bear vs a silverback gorilla** (two heavyweights, no numbers, confident opposing opinions).
5. **5 men vs 1 professional fighter** (skill against numbers).
6. **You vs the Walking Button** (callback gag): the player against the indestructible **walking button** from Chapter 2, reused whole (it comes alive as a cheeky, offended fighter). The imp needles that the player is not as sure as they want to be, given the button's track record (shot, pressed, cursed, charged out of the dark, always fine). Played for laughs.
7. **100 toddlers vs 1 adult man** (absurd and genuinely dividing, dark-comic; the toddlers drawn as small on-model figures).
8. **1 gorilla vs 1 gorilla, but one has a knife** (modifier gag): identical fighters, one small **red** ink knife; makes people argue how much the knife actually matters.

## Chapter 7 (The Confession Booth)
The chapter where **the dead judge finally does his actual job.** A **fixed booth** scene: dead souls file in **one at a time**, confess an interpersonal "am I the asshole" style dispute with **no clean answer**, and the player passes a **verdict** on them. Then the crowd reveal shows whether people **agreed with the player's verdict**. It is the judge chapter, the confession chapter, and the crowd chapter at once, with the **crowd split front and centre as the central hook**. The disputes **escalate from petty to genuinely hard**, and each one **genuinely splits people** (a lopsided case would be cut, the same one rule as every question).

**The imp's role shifts here: he is the court clerk / bailiff, not the one posing dilemmas** (see `narrative.md`). He announces each case off the docket, moves things along, heckles the player's rulings, and notes (choice-independent, never grading as morally right or wrong) when the crowd thinks the player ruled strangely. A fresh register for him: a bored, snide court official. He still **mocks whatever the player rules and never grades it** (the same rule as every chapter); who is the asshole has no correct answer, and he says as much.

**The verdict mechanic (three options, not a binary).** Each case is judged with **three** verdicts, because these disputes have an "everyone sucks here" middle. For each case the player picks one of: the confessor **is in the wrong** (the asshole), the confessor is **not in the wrong** (the other party is), or **both are in the wrong / no one is clean** (the everyone-sucks-here / forgiven-but-messy middle). It reuses the **multiple-choice** idea (pick one of three) as three verdict affordances clear of the scene, labelled naturally per case (the exact wording varies). Whatever the player rules, the imp reacts **choice-independent** (mocking the ruling, or noting the crowd disagreed), never declaring a correct verdict, and a **seeded three-way crowd split** is shown on the reveal.

**Presentation** (see `art-and-ui.md`): a fixed **confession booth** drawn once and held for the whole chapter (the docket's conveyor of cases files in front of it, so it reads as a fixed location, not a fresh scene per beat). Each confessor is an on-model ink **figure** who **files in** from one side, stands in the booth doorway, and confesses; the imp announces the case and the confessor states their side in the **dialogue box**, with a short **floating headline** over the confessor (their core position, the no-bubble ink-over-a-figure treatment). The player rules via the **three verdict affordances** (clear of the scene, in the choice-affordance row above the dialogue box). The confessor **files out** after the verdict and the next files in. Light animated ink consequence per case, scaled light (files in, files out).

**The ten cases, in order** (petty to heavy; each an "am I the asshole" dispute that genuinely splits; themes one step off any named people or events; all no-right-answer):
1. **The Recliner** — a man reclined his seat on a long flight into the knees of the person behind him and will not apologise, he paid for the seat. The opener that teaches the verdict loop. [light]
2. **The Wedding** — a woman wore white to someone else's wedding on purpose, to spite a bride she has history with. A crime, or was the bride asking for it. [light]
3. **The Loan** — a man lent his struggling brother money, then shamed him publicly at a family dinner when he could not repay on time. [medium]
4. **The Button's Confession** (the gag, the mid-chapter breather) — the **walking button** from earlier chapters files into the booth and confesses, with total sincerity, to something absurdly trivial (it bumped a man in a doorway and did not say sorry), and earnestly demands the player's verdict. The imp treats it as a normal case on the docket; the three-verdict mechanic still applies (you rule on the button). Comic relief on the bench. [light, gag]
5. **The Secret** — a woman found out her best friend's partner was cheating and said nothing, deciding it was not her business; the friend later found out she knew. Silence as betrayal, or minding your own business. [medium]
6. **The Promise** — a man promised his dying mother he would keep the family home forever, then sold it a year later because he genuinely needed the money to feed his own kids. A promise to the dead against the needs of the living. [heavy]
7. **The Favorite** — a parent openly favored one child their whole life; the neglected one cut them off and refused to visit as they died. Does a lifetime of favoritism justify abandonment at the end. [heavy]
8. **The Good Deed** — a man saved a stranger's life, then held it over them for years, demanding gratitude and favors forever. Is a good deed still good if it becomes a debt you collect on. [medium]
9. **The Whistle** — a woman reported a beloved coworker for something genuinely wrong, and the whole workplace turned on HER; the coworker lost everything, and she asks if she was the asshole for being right. Doing the right thing and paying for it. [heavy]
10. **The Two Stories** (the closer, the one variant) — **two** souls file in together, the two sides of the same dispute, and each tells the same events completely differently, both sincere, both convinced they were wronged. The player must rule (believe her, believe him, or no knowing) **without knowing whose version is true, and never finds out.** Contradictory testimony, the judge's real nightmare, and a fitting close to the docket. [heavy]

**Stats and profile.** Each case seeds a **three-way crowd split** shown on the reveal, made to feel like real "am I the asshole" comment-section splits (often close, sometimes with a big "everyone sucks" or "not the asshole" share). The profile is fed **silently by the pattern of the player's verdicts** (first pass: mapped to **mercy vs. justice**, where a verdict condemning the confessor leans justice / harsh and one absolving them leans mercy / lenient, and the two-stories closer reads decisiveness instead, handing down a verdict vs. refusing to; see `narrative.md`, `open-questions.md`), never shown. **Seven** of the ten cases feed the profile; the petty opener, the wedding, and the button gag are **stats-only**. It is a **central-hook (crowd) chapter**: the crowd reveal is the payoff every case ends on.

**Breathers.** The chapter is medium-toned; the **button gag (case 4) is the built-in breather.** Optional would-you-rather breathers from the shared pool could interleave lightly, but the chapter is deliberately **not overloaded**, so none are added this pass beyond the gag (see `open-questions.md`).

**No secret ending, no fail state, no scoring, profile silent** (as every chapter). **Placement (the Chapter 6 gap):** this is built as **Chapter 7** with Chapter 6 not yet made, so for now it is **reachable via the dev picker** and is in Build Settings, but Chapter 5 is **not** chained into it (Chapter 5 still holds at its end); wiring it into the run is a one-line hand-off once Chapter 6 lands (see `tech.md`, `open-questions.md`).
